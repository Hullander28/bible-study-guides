tagline: Faithful in exile under the Most High who rules the kingdoms of men
author: Daniel, a Judean taken to Babylon as a youth, who served in the courts of Babylon and Persia (7:1; 9:2; 12:4; Matthew 24:15).
date: Events from 605 BC (the first deportation) to about 536 BC (the third year of Cyrus), written in the 6th century BC.
audience: Jewish exiles in Babylon and God's people living under hostile world powers in every age.
setting: Babylon and Persia, in the courts of Nebuchadnezzar, Belshazzar, Darius the Mede, and Cyrus.
purpose: To show that the God of Israel is sovereign over all kingdoms, to encourage faithfulness under pressure, and to reveal that God's everlasting kingdom will triumph through the Son of Man.
structure: Stories of Daniel and his friends in the foreign court (1-6) and Daniel's apocalyptic visions of world kingdoms and God's final kingdom (7-12). Chapters 2:4b-7:28 are written in Aramaic.

## themes
- God's Sovereignty Over Nations | "The Most High rules the kingdom of men and gives it to whom he will" (4:17).
- Faithfulness in a Foreign Culture | Daniel and his friends serve well while refusing to compromise.
- God's Deliverance | God rescues from the furnace and the lions, and even when He doesn't, His people stay faithful (3:18).
- Pride and Humility | Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar learn that God humbles the proud.
- Prayer | Daniel prays regularly, boldly, and with confession.
- The Coming Kingdom | God's kingdom, like a stone not cut by human hands, will fill the earth.
- The Son of Man | One like a son of man receives an everlasting dominion (7:13-14).
- Resurrection | Daniel 12:2 clearly teaches the resurrection of the dead.

## christ
- 7:13-14 | Jesus' favorite title, "Son of Man," comes from this vision; He claimed it at His trial (Mark 14:62).
- 2:34-35, 44 | The stone cut without hands is Christ's kingdom crushing earthly empires.
- 3:25 | A fourth figure in the fire pictures God's presence with His people in suffering.
- 9:25-26 | An anointed one is "cut off" after the appointed weeks, pointing to the Messiah's death.
- 12:2 | Resurrection to everlasting life fulfilled through Christ (John 5:28-29).

- How does 3:17-18 shape your faith when God might not deliver in the way you hope?
- What does Nebuchadnezzar's story teach you about pride?
- Daniel prayed three times a day even when it was illegal. What does your prayer life look like under pressure?
- How does the vision of the Son of Man receiving an eternal kingdom give you hope today?

@ Daniel's Resolve
Nebuchadnezzar takes young nobles from Judah, including Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, to train in Babylon and renames them. Daniel resolves not to defile himself with the king's food and proposes a ten-day test of vegetables and water. God gives them favor, knowledge, and wisdom ten times better than all the magicians.

? How did Daniel show wisdom and respect in his request (1:8-14)?
? Who gave the young men their success (1:9, 17)?

@ The Dream of the Great Statue
Nebuchadnezzar demands his wise men tell him his dream and its meaning. Daniel and his friends pray, and God reveals the mystery. Daniel blesses God, who "changes times and seasons." The statue of gold, silver, bronze, iron, and clay represents successive kingdoms, struck by a stone cut without hands that becomes a mountain filling the earth: God's eternal kingdom.

? What does Daniel's prayer reveal about God (2:20-23)?

@ The Fiery Furnace
Nebuchadnezzar sets up a golden image and commands all to worship it. Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse: "Our God... is able to deliver us... But if not... we will not serve your gods." They are thrown into the furnace, but a fourth figure walks with them, and they come out unharmed, not even smelling of smoke.

@ Nebuchadnezzar Humbled
Nebuchadnezzar dreams of a great tree cut down. Daniel interprets: the king will lose his sanity and live like an animal until he acknowledges that the Most High rules. A year later, boasting over Babylon, he is struck. When his reason returns, he praises God: "Those who walk in pride he is able to humble."

@ The Writing on the Wall
At a feast, King Belshazzar drinks from the temple vessels and praises idols. A hand writes on the wall. Daniel interprets: "Mene, Mene, Tekel, and Parsin." God has numbered the kingdom; Belshazzar is weighed and found wanting. That night he is killed, and Darius the Mede takes the kingdom.

@ Daniel in the Lions' Den
Officials, jealous of Daniel, trick Darius into banning prayer to anyone but the king for thirty days. Daniel keeps praying three times a day toward Jerusalem. He is thrown to the lions, but God shuts their mouths. Darius decrees that all should fear Daniel's God, "for he is the living God."

A boastful horn wars against the saints, but the kingdom is given to the saints of the Most High.

Daniel sees a ram with two horns (Media and Persia) trampled by a goat with one great horn (Greece). The horn breaks into four, and from one comes a little horn that exalts itself, attacks God's people, and stops the sacrifices. Gabriel explains, and Daniel is overcome.
